01 / THE DISCOVERY

On the edge
of the Atlantic.

In December 1900, three men were stationed on a small, isolated island west of Scotland. Their job was simple, and vital: keep the lighthouse burning so ships could navigate the dangerous waters around the Outer Hebrides.

James Ducat, Thomas Marshall and Donald McArthur were on duty at the Flannan Isles Lighthouse on Eilean Mòr. The island was remote, the weather could turn brutal, and there was no radio link to the mainland.

On 26 December, the lighthouse tender Hesperus arrived for the routine relief. The lightkeepers did not appear. A rocket was fired. No one answered.

“A dreadful accident has happened at Flannans. The three Keepers ... have disappeared from the Island.”— Telegram from the master of Hesperus, 26 December 1900, reproduced by the Northern Lighthouse Board

Relief keeper Joseph Moore went ashore and climbed to the station. He found it empty. The lighthouse was in order. The lamps were prepared. The kitchen was tidy. Three men had vanished from their post, and the island offered no clear explanation.

THE CENTRAL QUESTIONWhat could make three experienced keepers leave a functioning lighthouse — and never return?
02 / TIMELINE

The last known days.

7 DEC 1900
Last relief before the disappearance

The keepers were left on Eilean Mòr. Joseph Moore remained ashore on Lewis.

13 DEC 1900
Last written log entries

The last written entries in the official keeper's log were dated 13 December. Later observations were noted on a slate for transfer.

15 DEC 1900
The light was reportedly absent

A passing vessel noted that the lighthouse light was not visible around midnight. The report did not reach the Commissioners in time to trigger an immediate response.

26 DEC 1900
The empty station discovered

The tender Hesperus reached the island. Moore searched the station and found no keepers.

8 JAN 1901
Official report submitted

Superintendent Robert Muirhead documented the evidence and proposed that a large wave had swept the men away near the landing.

ARCHIVAL RECORD
ARCHIVE NOTE / MYTH CHECK

Popular retellings often quote dramatic final logbook entries about a crying keeper, a violent storm or men praying. The National Records of Scotland states that it does not hold the lighthouse logbook. Those famous lines should not be treated as authenticated entries from the surviving official record.

04 / COMPETING EXPLANATIONS

What might have happened?

THE OFFICIAL EXPLANATIONBEST SUPPORTED

A sudden, powerful wave

Superintendent Robert Muirhead concluded that the men likely went toward the west landing, perhaps to secure equipment or inspect storm damage, and were caught by a large unexpected wave. The damaged landing structures and missing outdoor clothing support this explanation.

ALTERNATIVE POSSIBILITYPLAUSIBLE, NOT PROVEN

One accident, then a rescue attempt

It is possible that one keeper went toward the landing and the others followed in an attempt to help. The surviving evidence cannot establish the precise sequence, so this remains a possible reconstruction rather than a documented fact.

POPULAR LEGENDSUNSUPPORTED

Something supernatural

Stories of ghosts, abduction and impossible events grew around the disappearance. They are part of the folklore, not explanations supported by the historical evidence examined here.

05 / FINAL ASSESSMENT
CASE
REVIEWED

The sea may have
kept the answer.

The most evidence-based explanation is that the three keepers were swept into the sea during dangerous conditions near the west landing. It fits the physical damage, the missing outdoor clothing and the official investigation.

But no bodies were recovered. No witness saw the final moments. The exact sequence of events remains unknown.

VERDICTUNRESOLVED FATEProbable accidental drowning · Exact circumstances unknown

Three men went out to tend a light.
Only the light was found again.
